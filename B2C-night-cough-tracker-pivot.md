# Pivot the night cough box toward parents

The bedside tracker should be sold to **parents of children (about 2–12 years) with asthma or a persistent night cough**, not to adults with chronic cough, COPD patients or the general allergy market. The children's segment is where the evidence lines up best. Healthy children rarely cough at night, while children with asthma often do. Night waking is one of only four items doctors use to grade asthma control. Parents' reports of night cough agree poorly with recordings. And a wet cough lasting more than four weeks triggers a specific guideline step in children. Adults with chronic cough cough **2.5–5x less at night than by day**, so a bedside-only device misses most of their cough. Danish COPD patients already get home telemonitoring free from their region. The "adjust humidity to reduce cough" promise also has to go. Cochrane reviews find humidified air does little for colds or croup. The defensible environmental message is about **damp, condensation and dust mites (humidity above about 50%), cold bedrooms and poor ventilation**. Commercially, Denmark alone holds only about 45–65k target families, so the plan has to be Nordic or DACH (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) from the start. Price benchmarks point to roughly **€99–199 for the device plus €40–80/year**, or a short rental prescribed by a paediatrician. That price, and whether doctors make better decisions with the data, are the two biggest unverified assumptions.

## Night counting makes sense for children, not for adults with chronic cough

The whole product depends on one question: do the target users actually cough at night, and is that night cough informative? The answer differs sharply by segment.

For **adults with chronic cough**, night counting captures only a minority of the cough. In home ambulatory recordings, median time spent coughing was 15.59 cough-seconds/hour by day against 2.94 at night, about 5.3x lower ([Thorax 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2092471)). A 24-hour automated study found 25 vs 10 coughs per patient-hour, about 2.5x ([ERJ 2008](https://publications.ersnet.org/lookup/doi/10.1183/09031936.00057407)). Adults also judge their own night cough reasonably well. Subjective scores tracked objective counts more closely at night than by day ([Thorax 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2092471)). In a small study, night-cough questionnaire scores correlated strongly with acoustic counts (ρ −0.78 to −0.87), even though people could not estimate the actual count ([J Voice 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2024.10.027)). For this group, a 24-hour phone app or wearable such as Hyfe is the better form factor. The bedside box loses there.

For **children**, the picture flips. Night cough in a healthy child is a meaningful deviation. Healthy school-age children cough about **11.3 times per 24 hours (range 1–34), and nocturnal coughing is unusual** ([PMC1511564](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1511564); search summary, peer-reviewed source not read in full). In children with asthma it is common. **34–40% of children with asthma have nocturnal awakenings**, against about 25% of all children ([PMC4237310](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4237310)). Older objective recordings found nocturnal cough in **all children recovering from an exacerbation and in 39% of children with stable asthma** ([Thorax, older study](https://thorax.bmj.com/lookup/ijlink/YTozOntzOjQ6InBhdGgiO3M6MTQ6Ii9sb29rdXAvaWpsaW5rIjtzOjU6InF1ZXJ5IjthOjQ6e3M6ODoibGlua1R5cGUiO3M6NDoiQUJTVCI7czoxMToiam91cm5hbENvZGUiO3M6MTI6ImFyY2hkaXNjaGlsZCI7czo1OiJyZXNpZCI7czoxMDoiNjIvMTAvMTAwMSI7czo0OiJhdG9tIjtzOjI1OiIvdGhvcmF4am5sLzU4LzExLzk3NC5hdG9tIjt9czo4OiJmcmFnbWVudCI7czowOiIiO30=)). In a conference abstract, **27% (22/81) of children with controlled or partly controlled asthma had more than 2 cough episodes on at least one monitored night** ([ERS 2022 abstract](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/60/suppl66/1588); conference abstract, weaker).

Overnight counts were higher in 34 children with asthma exacerbations than in 15 non-asthmatic children. They were also higher in severe than in moderate exacerbations, with spikes at sleep onset and on waking ([Prim Care Respir J](https://www.nature.com/articles/pcrj200838.pdf); search summary). A small older study of stable asthmatic children reported a median of **6 cough bouts per night (range 0–272)**, highest in the 2 hours after bedtime ([PMC1746522](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1746522); search summary, small n). At population level, the Leicester Respiratory Cohort found **isolated night cough without wheeze in 10% of 1-year-olds and 18% of older children** ([PMC7553117](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7553117); [PMC5443519](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5443519/)). A small Japanese study found that children with allergic asthma and post-nasal drip coughed about 138 times overnight against about 100 without it ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/postnasal-drip-linked-with-more-nighttime-coughing-in-children-with-allergic-asthma); trade press, weak).

The honest caveat is that **stable asthmatic children still cough more by day than by night** ([Thorax 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1746522); [PMC1793508](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1793508/?page=4)). The case for night counting in children is therefore not about volume. It rests on three facts: night is the window parents cannot observe, night waking is the item that decides the control grade, and night cough rises from a low baseline around attacks.

|---|---|---|---|
| Children with asthma / recurrent night cough | Common (34–40% wake at night); all at exacerbation; abnormal in healthy kids | Yes. It is the window parents miss and the GINA (Global Initiative for Asthma) control item | **Beachhead** |

| Allergic rhinitis, post-viral cough | Night burden is mostly congestion; post-viral cough is self-limiting | Low value | Drop |

## The problem: parents are asleep during the symptom that grades asthma

**Problem statement.** Parents of children with asthma or persistent cough cannot observe night cough. Yet night cough is the symptom that helps define asthma control and that often climbs before an attack. What parents report agrees poorly with recordings. GPs therefore adjust treatment, or decide on a wet-cough work-up, based on a four-week memory of nights the parent mostly slept through.

The clinical anchor is strong. GINA grades symptom control on four items over the past four weeks, and **any single night waking due to asthma counts**. One or two items mean "partly controlled", and three or four mean "uncontrolled" ([GINA-based table, PMC7462684](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7462684/table/t1a); [paediatric table, PMC9488919](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9488919/table/TB1)). Poor control is widespread. **About 1 in 10 children worldwide have asthma symptoms, and half of them are inadequately controlled** ([Global Asthma Report 2022](https://globalasthmareport.org/foreword/summaries.php)). In Denmark, the REASSESS Youth registry of **29,851 children on asthma treatment** found poor control in 6.4–25% depending on age band. **Only 24% of uncontrolled children were in specialist care, and 71% of high-dose inhaled-steroid users were managed in primary care** ([Respir Res 2023, PMC10304602](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10304602/)). The GP is the decision-maker, and the GP decides on recall.

That recall is poor at night. In the landmark study, parents reported cough on **66 of 105 nights, but recordings found it on 93** (kappa 0.30) ([Falconer et al., Pediatr Pulmonol 1993](https://doi.org/10.1002/ppul.1950150405)). In a 145-home community study, agreement between parent reports and recordings was **kappa 0.02–0.10** ([Wallaceburg study](https://dx.doi.org/10.1023/a:1007311912777)). Children's and parents' diaries matched cough meters well for daytime cough but **poorly for night-time cough** ([Chang et al., ERJ 1998](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/11/2/462)). The BTS (British Thoracic Society) cough guideline calls parental reporting unreliable "especially" for nocturnal cough ([Thorax BTS supplement](https://thorax.bmj.com/content/63/Suppl_3/iii1.long)). One caveat: the core studies are small (n=15–145) and date from 1993–1998. No modern replication with a reported kappa was found.

The family burden is also documented. Caregivers of children with frequent night symptoms miss more work and report more anxiety and depression ([PMC3481184](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3481184)). Some cope by co-sleeping and monitoring at night ([PMC10340127](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10340127)).

## The solution: a no-audio bedside log that speaks the GP's language

The product is a bedside box with no wearable and no phone in bed. It counts coughs on-device, keeps no audio, labels each cough as probably wet or dry, and senses the bedroom environment. Its output is a one-page four-week report built around two questions doctors already ask. The first is "how many nights with cough or waking in the last 4 weeks, and is the trend rising?", which maps to the GINA and C-ACT (Childhood Asthma Control Test) night item. The second is "wet cough on how many of the last 28 nights?", which maps to the CHEST guideline. Under that guideline, unexplained wet cough lasting more than four weeks in a child leads to a two-week antibiotic course, and investigation follows if it persists ([CHEST guideline](https://seek.chestnet.org/AssetListing/CHEST-Guidelines-14139/Management-of-Children-With-Chronic-Wet-Cough-and-Protracted-Bacterial-Bronchitis-76208); [AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2019/0515/p654.pdf)). The report must be a summary, not raw data. Manually reviewing a single night's recording took a mean of **14 minutes** in one study ([Lindenhofer 2020, PMC7733140](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7733140/)).

**Value for the family** is peace of mind and evidence to bring to the appointment. Parents see whether last night was actually bad, whether this week is worse than last, and when a persistent wet cough is worth a GP visit. That is a care-seeking prompt, not a diagnosis. **Value for the doctor** is an objective, longitudinal view of exactly the window recall misses. Night cough baselines are low (often under 1 cough/hour), so relative rises stand out. Early data suggest nocturnal cough and respiratory rate begin rising **up to 5 days before asthma attacks** in children ([Albus CHAMP, Clinical Trials Arena](https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/news/albus-demonstrates-device-ability-to-detect-asthma-attacks-in-children/); company-reported conference data, weak). In COPD, cough-only alerts fired a mean of 3.4 days before 45% of exacerbations ([Lung 2021, PMC8053154](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053154)). Doctors are open to this kind of data. In an AMA/Medscape survey of 2,222 physicians in 6 countries, **97% review wearable data in some form, but only about 28% (US) act on it**. The barriers are workflow and accuracy ([AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/press-center/ama-press-releases/wearable-data-adoption-stalled-system-barriers)). The honest limit is that **no trial shows cough monitoring improves clinical decisions or outcomes**. "Better decisions" is the hypothesis, not the evidence.

### Reshape the bedroom advice: damp and cold, not "add humidity"

The original idea, "change humidity or temperature to reduce night cough", is not supported. Cochrane found low-quality, inconsistent evidence for heated humidified air in colds ([PubMed 28849871](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28849871/)). For croup, the croup score "probably does not improve greatly" with humidified air ([PubMed 16855994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16855994/)). Dry-air studies measure eye and airway irritation in offices, not cough ([PubMed 38154254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38154254/)). **No study was found that measured night cough counts against bedroom humidity or temperature directly.**

What the evidence does support is indirect, and it fits Danish homes. **Home dampness and mould raise asthma outcomes by about 30–50%** ([Fisk et al. meta-analysis](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/924852)). WHO judged the association between dampness and asthma exacerbation sufficient ([via PMC3114807](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3114807)). Keeping mean relative humidity **below about 50% restricts dust mites** and cut mite allergen over 17 months in temperate homes ([Arlian 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11149998/)). In a Danish survey, **40.1% reported condensation on the inside of bedroom windows in winter**, which signals too much moisture, not too little ([PMC7034447](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7034447)). Cold also matters. In the New Zealand housing trial, bedroom temperature affected lung function in asthmatic children, with the largest effects **below 12 °C**, lagged over 0–12 days ([JECH 2013](https://jech.bmj.com/content/early/2013/08/12/jech-2013-202632.full.pdf)). A small Vienna study linked night cough to lower bedroom temperature ([PMC7733140](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7733140/)).

The reshaped advice engine therefore alerts on four conditions. The first is sustained humidity above 50–60% or a condensation risk, with the advice to ventilate, avoid drying laundry indoors and check for mould. The second is a bedroom below about 12–16 °C, judged on a multi-day trend. The third is CO2 above about 1000 ppm as a ventilation proxy; 45.6% of Shanghai children's bedrooms exceeded it ([Indoor Air](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/ina.12742)), but its link is to rhinitis, not cough. The fourth, optionally, is PM2.5 (fine particulate matter), with a HEPA purifier suggestion; HEPA cut bedroom PM2.5 by 63% in a paediatric crossover trial ([JAMA Pediatrics 2020](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2763826)). The engine should **never recommend a humidifier for cough**, because over-humidifying feeds mites and mould. CO2 and PM2.5 require sensors the current build does not have.

## Target customer and moat: own the paediatric night report and its channel

The **beachhead customer** is a Nordic or German parent of a 2–12-year-old with diagnosed asthma or recurrent night cough, sent home from the GP with "keep an eye on the nights". This family already buys night-time reassurance. Baby-monitor parents pay €290–€413 for hardware plus €80–€300/year for subscriptions ([Nanit](https://www.nanit.com/pricing); [Owlet360](https://owletcare.com/pages/owlet360)). One caution: marketing explicitly to "asthmatic children" signals a medical intended purpose under EU MDR ([Johner on Rule 11](https://www.johner-institute.com/articles/regulatory-affairs/mdr-rule-11-software/)). The launch framing must be a "family night cough and sleep log, share it with your doctor", without the words detect, alert, diagnose or exacerbation.

No shipping consumer product combines a standalone bedside box, wet/dry labelling, bedroom environment correlation, a doctor-ready report and no stored audio.

## The market is tens of millions of euros, and willingness to pay is unproven

Top-down "cough monitoring" market reports disagree wildly, from $0.42 B to $1.2 B ([Dataintelo](https://dataintelo.com/report/cough-monitoring-devices-market); [Market Intelo](https://marketintelo.com/report/cough-monitoring-devices-market)). They are low-tier and should not be pitched. Bottom-up is more honest. Denmark has **more than 400,000 people on asthma medication a year** ([Clin Epidemiol 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5094569)). Child asthma prevalence there is about **11.7%** ([Respir Res 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s12931-023-02482-7)). Applied to about 0.72 M Danish children aged 0–11, that gives roughly **45–65k target families (estimate)**. At 10% adoption, €149 per device and €50/year, that is only about €0.8 M in devices and €0.3 M/year recurring. Scaled across the Nordics (×4.6), the pool is about 230–320k children with asthma, worth roughly €4–5 M in devices at 10% adoption. EU-wide, perhaps 5–8 M children have asthma. A 1% paying share at €149 plus €50/year is about €7–12 M in devices plus €2.5–4 M/year (all estimates).

Willingness to pay is the weakest number in the case. The price envelope comes from adjacent products. Phone cough apps anchor at about €30/year, and baby monitors at €80–€300/year plus hardware ([App Store DK](https://apps.apple.com/dk/app/coughpro/id1505735530?l=da); [Nanit](https://www.nanit.com/pricing)). The only direct signal is negative. In a 2026 pilot of a home air-quality monitor for asthmatic children, **only about half of interviewed families said they would use it if they had to pay** ([JMIR Pediatrics 2026](https://pediatrics.jmir.org/2026/1/e92070); n=7 interviewed). Retention is a second threat, because **a median 70% of users abandon health apps within 100 days** ([JMIR 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11694054/)). Asthma flares and wet-cough episodes are episodic, so a **4-week "objectify the nights before your appointment" rental, recommended by the GP or paediatrician**, fits the need better than an annual subscription. That rental model is a hypothesis to test with a Van Westendorp price survey through Astma-Allergi Danmark.

## Key risks and unverified assumptions

The biggest risk is **accuracy at low counts**. Phone-based detectors produce about **1.03 false positives per hour** ([Hyfe validation, Sci Rep 2025](https://doaj.org/article/a19cc385064a4c1ebd5d233c5095ce85)). Over an 8-hour night that is about 8 spurious coughs, which can swamp a child's true baseline. Separating the patient's coughs from a partner's reached only **83%** accuracy ([JMIR 2020](https://www.jmir.org/2020/7/e18082)), and sibling-shared rooms are common. No cough validation in children's bedrooms exists. **Wet/dry classification from a bedside microphone is unproven.** Published 75–88% figures come from close-mic recordings ([PMC545936](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC545936); [Swarnkar 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-013-0741-6)), the one public open benchmark reached only AUC ~0.65 ([GitHub](https://github.com/fishyville/WetDryCoughDetector)), and even clinicians reach only about 0.75–0.79 against bronchoscopy. The label must be shown as "indicative".

The decision-impact claim is unverified, because no study shows that doctors change or improve decisions using cough data. The recall evidence is old and small. Willingness to pay and retention are unmeasured for this product. Regulation pulls in two directions: a medical-sounding claim is what sells to parents and doctors, but it triggers MDR Class IIa. A "no audio" design also limits the dataset moat. Large placebo responses in environmental trials ([LASER RCT](https://www.phc.ox.ac.uk/publications/1023048)) mean apparent "improvement after advice" may be regression to the mean. The Danish market is too small to sustain the company alone. The CO2 and PM2.5 advice needs hardware the current build lacks. Two inferences in the notes were not verified against primary text and should be checked before external use: GDPR Article 9 treatment of derived cough counts, and the exact C-ACT item wording.

## Conclusion

The data do not support the original broad pitch of a cough tracker for everyone with long-term cough that tunes humidity. They support something narrower and stronger: an **instrument for the night-time blind spot in paediatric asthma care**. Healthy children rarely cough at night. In asthmatic children, night cough is both common and the item that decides the control grade, and parents measurably miss it. The environmental module earns its place as a **damp, mould and cold detector for Nordic bedrooms**, not as a cough cure.

The next two experiments decide the business: a two-week validation in 10–20 children's bedrooms against manual counts, and a price survey of Danish asthma parents. If false positives at low counts cannot be controlled, the report loses its credibility with doctors, and no channel or moat will rescue it.
