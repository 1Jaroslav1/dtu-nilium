# Objective cough count, timing, and strength

## Do daily cough counts distinguish health from individual diseases?

### Takeaway

Group averages often differ, with substantial individual overlap and large differences across study populations. Frequency is best established as an objective measure of symptom burden and its evolution.

### Cited Findings

- Holt & Smith (2023) recorded 79 healthy volunteers for 24 hours with validated VitaloJAK ambulatory audio, with trained counters tagging individual explosive sounds after filtering. The geometric mean was 4.6 coughs over 24 hours, and the range was 0–136. Most occurred during the day. [How often do healthy people cough?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10638680/)
- Lodhi et al. (2019) analyzed recordings from 92 participants with mild to moderate asthma and 44 healthy controls. Median counts were 27 (IQR 12–67) vs 3.5 (IQR 1–19.75) coughs/24 h, respectively. Both groups coughed more in the day. The asthma group had a morning increase at 08:00–08:59 and a trough around 02:00–05:59. Asthma and healthy groups differed in age despite approximate matching in recruitment. [Cough rhythms in asthma](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6619487/)
- Yousaf et al. (2013) observed geometric means of 18.6 coughs/24 h for healthy adults and 275 for patients with a mix of respiratory conditions, with a similar day/night pattern. Rounded geometric means for individual disease subgroups in Table 1 were 477 for unexplained chronic cough, 321 for cough-variant asthma/eosinophilic bronchitis, 213 for COPD, 107 for asthma, and 128 for ACE inhibitor-associated cough. These are selected study groups and should not be generalized as cutoffs or typical counts for every patient. [Cough frequency in health and disease](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/41/1/241)

### Inferences

- Asthma and healthy count ranges overlap even in the same study. The different healthy geometric means across two studies also argue against treating a single number as a universal disease threshold. [Lodhi et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6619487/) [Holt & Smith](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10638680/) [Yousaf et al.](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/41/1/241)

### Gaps

- These studies do not validate a count cutoff to identify an individual disease in a broadly representative symptomatic population.

## Can a change in count track illness, recovery, or a flare?

### Takeaway

Objective counts can measure change in an existing condition. The relationship to overall disease severity varies by illness, study population, and outcome measured.

### Cited Findings

- Sunger et al. (2013) recorded 54 otherwise healthy volunteers with acute cough and symptoms suggestive of a viral upper respiratory tract infection over two consecutive 24-hour sessions. Geometric mean rates fell from 12.1 to 9.0 coughs/hour. In the same study, 72.4% reported sneezing and 63.8% described productive cough. The eligibility criteria relied on symptoms suggestive of infection, so these figures do not establish viral versus bacterial specificity. [Objective measurement of cough in otherwise healthy volunteers with acute cough](https://doi.org/10.1183/09031936.00190111)
- Boesch et al. (2023) monitored 46 adults hospitalized with pneumonia (32 with COVID-19, 14 with other pneumonia). Hourly counts generally fell with recovery and correlated with markers of inflammation and oxygenation. Cough trajectories were similar between COVID-19 and non-COVID pneumonia (incidence rate ratio 1.07, 95% CI 0.86–1.33), with high variation between patients. Single-centre and exploratory study. [Smartphone-based cough monitoring as a near real-time digital pneumonia biomarker](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10152266/)
- Altshuler et al. (2023) enrolled 123 hospitalized COVID-19 patients at two centres. Higher early cough rates were associated with more favorable outcomes, with an AUC of 0.761 for the full monitoring period. Poor outcome was intubation or death, and that study excluded patients requiring rapid intubation on admission. Overall reported AUC is safe to use, but early-window values in the abstract and body are presented inconsistently, so avoid quoting those. [Digital cough monitoring in hospitalized COVID-19 patients](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9827741/)
- Morice et al. (2025) enrolled 40 people with COPD in a longitudinal study of passive, nighttime cough monitoring. Thirty-eight completed the study, 41 exacerbations occurred, and 32 of the 38 had adequate data for personalization and alerting. The system flagged 59% of exacerbations and the authors estimated about 30% of exacerbations did not involve a cough increase. [Can Passive Cough Monitoring Predict COPD Exacerbations?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40223505/)
- Gabaldón-Figueira et al. (2022) analyzed smartphone data from 616 participants and 62,325 recorded coughs. Cough varied substantially within a day and across days. Simulating 24-hour monitoring illustrated that estimates could miss or misstate longitudinal changes, especially at low rates or small changes. This was a prospective database analysis with simulation, including case analyses on a small subset. [Longitudinal passive cough monitoring and its implications for detecting changes in clinical status](https://doi.org/10.1183/23120541.00001-2022)

### Inferences

- Counts are often more informative relative to a person's recent baseline or recovery trajectory. For acute upper respiratory infection and pneumonia the observed decreases were on average, and COPD monitoring missed some exacerbations. [Sunger et al.](https://doi.org/10.1183/09031936.00190111) [Boesch et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10152266/) [Morice et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40223505/)
- The exploratory pneumonia and COVID-19 studies assessed different endpoints. A declining count during recovery in one sample and an association of lower early count with poorer outcomes in another illustrate why the count is not a universally monotonic measure of how sick a patient is. [Boesch et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10152266/) [Altshuler et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9827741/)

### Gaps

- Longitudinal data support monitoring in specific populations. The studies do not establish a single general-purpose threshold or a universal observation length.

## What does cough intensity measure, and how does it relate to severity?

### Takeaway

Cough strength can be measured physiologically, and acoustic strength can approximate it under standardized conditions. Patient-reported symptom severity and underlying disease severity convey different information from either strength or frequency.

### Cited Findings

- Lee et al. (2017) studied 32 people total with chronic cough and healthy controls. Sound power and peak energy of voluntary coughs correlated with peak flow (median Spearman rho 0.87–0.88) and esophageal pressure (0.89). Microphone position affected measured sound power and energy. The results validate an acoustic proxy under controlled recording conditions. [Sound: a non-invasive measure of cough intensity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5501240/)
- Lee et al. (2016) found greater maximum voluntary cough intensity in patients with chronic cough compared with healthy controls by esophageal/gastric pressure and peak flow. Spontaneous cough pressure was significantly lower than maximum voluntary cough pressure. Within participants, subjective intensity and physiological intensity correlations were 0.82–0.86. [The Intensity of Voluntary, Induced, and Spontaneous Cough](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26134718/)
- In the Sunger acute cough study, objective cough counts and visual analogue scores had correlations of 0.28–0.59. Counts declined significantly on the second day while patient-rated cough severity did not. [Objective measurement of cough in otherwise healthy volunteers with acute cough](https://doi.org/10.1183/09031936.00190111)
- Schelfhout et al. (2022) analyzed phase 2b trial data for refractory or unexplained chronic cough. Objective 24-hour frequency and patient-reported outcomes were weak to moderately correlated (Spearman rho 0.30–0.58 in absolute value; for patient-rated intensity, 0.40–0.55). Participants who felt minimally improved had about a 30% lower rate, while those reporting much or very much improvement averaged around 55% reduction. Authors proposed a 30% within-patient reduction for treatment-response assessment in chronic cough trials. [Validation and Meaningful Change Thresholds for an Objective Cough Frequency Measurement in Chronic Cough](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9675653/)

### Inferences

- A loud voluntary recording from a close microphone can be stronger as a sound because of recording distance and collection method. Decibels from casual phone recordings cannot be interpreted as a portable disease severity scale based on these studies. [Lee et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5501240/) [Lee et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26134718/)
- Count and symptom impact are complementary. A person can improve on one measure without the other moving to the same extent. [Schelfhout et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9675653/) [Sunger et al.](https://doi.org/10.1183/09031936.00190111)

### Gaps

- These physiological intensity studies do not establish everyday cough loudness thresholds for distinguishing allergic, infectious, or other causes. Their controlled nature also limits extrapolation to spontaneous free-living recordings.
