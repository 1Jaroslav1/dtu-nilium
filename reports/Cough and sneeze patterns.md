# What cough and sneeze patterns reveal

**Cough counts are most useful for tracking how a person’s symptoms change over time. Sneezing is a clue to allergy when it comes with itching or watery eyes and recurs after a recognizable exposure.** Daily counts overlap across healthy people and people with respiratory conditions. Sneezing is also common in several confirmed viral infections. A few cough patterns, such as a bark with hoarseness and noisy breathing in a young child, can help identify a clinical syndrome. Evidence for identifying a particular disease by count, loudness, or sound alone remains limited. [Holt & Smith, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10638680/) · [International Consensus Statement on Allergic Rhinitis, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/alr.23090) · [Geismar et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10397315/#Tab1) · [Canadian Paediatric Society croup guidance](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/acute-management-of-croup)

## Daily cough counts overlap between groups

Holt and Smith recorded 79 healthy adults for 24 hours. The geometric mean was **4.6 coughs per day**, with individual counts ranging from **0 to 136**. In a separate study by Lodhi and colleagues, the median was **27 coughs per day in people with mild to moderate asthma versus 3.5 in healthy controls**. The middle half of the asthma group recorded 12–67 coughs, overlapping with the healthy group’s 1–19.75. Both groups coughed less overnight. A count in the overlap tells you little about whether an individual has asthma. [Holt & Smith, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10638680/) · [Lodhi et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6619487/)

Counts can reveal a change during a known illness. In 54 volunteers with acute cough and symptoms suggestive of an upper respiratory infection, the geometric mean fell from **12.1 to 9.0 coughs per hour** across consecutive 24-hour recordings. In a 46-person hospital study, cough declined during recovery from pneumonia and followed similar trajectories in patients with and without COVID-19. A passive monitor study in people with COPD flagged **59% of flare-ups**. A separate hospital study found that higher early cough rates in COVID-19 were associated with better outcomes, illustrating that the relationship between count and overall illness severity depends on the population and the outcome. [Sunger et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1183/09031936.00190111) · [Boesch et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10152266/) · [Morice et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40223505/) · [Altshuler et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9827741/)

## Sneezing points toward allergy when symptoms follow exposure

In a study of 80 people who considered themselves free of rhinitis, participants logged an average of **1.2 sneezes per day** over two weeks. More than 95% averaged fewer than four. This describes one volunteer sample. In a controlled study of 18 people with known ragweed allergy, increasing doses of ragweed allergen elicited a reproducible sneezing response that differed from placebo. In 1,511 adults evaluated for rhinitis, sneezing was associated with **roughly four times the odds of allergic versus nonallergic rhinitis** after adjustment. Itching and eye symptoms were also associated with allergy. That comparison applies to people with rhinitis who underwent allergy evaluation. [Hansen & Mygind, 2002](https://www.rhinologyjournal.com/Rhinology_issues/113.pdf) · [Doyle et al., 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7697479/) · [Di Lorenzo et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1159/000320050)

Sneezing also occurred in **90% of rhinovirus illnesses, 90% of seasonal coronavirus illnesses, 77% of RSV illnesses, and 76% of influenza illnesses** in a comparison of confirmed cases from household cohorts in England and Wales. In periods dominated by different Omicron variants, 49–60% of COVID-19 cases reported sneezing. Cough was common across the four non-COVID viruses too, occurring in 79–91% of illnesses. These figures describe the share of illnesses with each symptom. They give little information about how many times an individual coughed or sneezed, or how the two counts were related. [Geismar et al., 2023, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10397315/#Tab1)

There is limited evidence that the number of sneezes in a row, sneeze loudness or force, or a wet versus dry sneeze can distinguish allergy, infection, or irritation. In one experiment, listeners performed at chance level when asked to tell whether recordings of coughs and sneezes came from people who reported being ill. The experiment used self-reported illness and measured what listeners could judge from the recordings. [International Consensus Statement on Allergic Rhinitis, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/alr.23090) · [Michalak et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7341931/)

## Cough type helps most in specific clinical settings

Wet cough can indicate mucus in the airways. In a study of hospitalized children, listeners agreed strongly on wet versus dry cough, but diagnoses from the recordings agreed only slightly with discharge diagnoses for conditions including asthma, bronchiolitis and pneumonia. Duration changes the interpretation. A persistent wet cough for more than four weeks in a child is a recognized clue for protracted bacterial bronchitis when other criteria fit. In adults with acute cough, yellow or green phlegm provided little evidence for a bacterial cause in a primary-care study. [Chang et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15638942/) · [Bisballe-Müller et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2020.06.067) · [European Respiratory Society chronic cough guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6942543/) · [Altiner et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19242860/)

A young child with a **seal-like bark, hoarseness and a harsh sound when breathing in** has the classic symptom cluster for croup, which several viruses can cause. In a study of adolescents and adults tested for pertussis, **90% of confirmed cases had coughing fits**, but so did about **75% of the comparison group**. An inhaled whoop after coughing was present in **19% of confirmed cases and 14% of the comparison group**. Vomiting after a coughing fit is another clue. These features shift suspicion and many confirmed cases lack the whoop. [Canadian Paediatric Society croup guidance](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/acute-management-of-croup) · [Miyashita et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3600016/) · [CHEST pertussis guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6859243/#tbl5)

## Loudness, physical strength and symptom burden measure different things

In a 32-person lab study, the sound power of voluntary coughs correlated strongly with peak airflow and pressure, and the microphone’s position changed the measured sound. In a separate chronic cough trial analysis, daily cough frequency had only weak-to-moderate correlations with how severe, intense or disruptive people rated their symptoms. A microphone can capture aspects of the physical effort behind a cough. How much it disrupts someone’s sleep or daily life, and how serious their underlying illness is, require other information. [Lee et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5501240/) · [Schelfhout et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9675653/)

Even automated sound analysis needs to be tested against realistic alternatives. In a study of **67,842 people** with PCR results, a COVID-19 model using respiratory audio, including cough, scored **0.85 before adjustment and 0.62 after matching for age, sex or gender, and reported symptoms**. The score was ROC-AUC, where 0.50 means chance discrimination. The drop shows how much apparent diagnostic performance can depend on who ends up in each group. [Coppock et al., 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-023-00773-8)

## What makes a comparison meaningful

Studies can count individual cough sounds or group several into one bout. A coughing fit can therefore contribute several events to one study’s total and a single bout to another’s. Reports that someone sneezed at all answer a different question from diaries that record every sneeze. To tell whether a pattern identifies a disease, the strongest design would use consistent measurements and test people with similar symptoms who have confirmed competing causes, including allergy, irritant rhinitis and respiratory infections. The research above provides useful pieces of that comparison, with the clearest evidence for frequency as a measure of symptom change. [Holt & Smith, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10638680/) · [Boesch et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10152266/) · [Geismar et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10397315/#Tab1) · [Coppock et al., 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-023-00773-8)
